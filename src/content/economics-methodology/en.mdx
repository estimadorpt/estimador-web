# How will we read the economy?

Nothing here is a current reading of the economy. The page explains what the section will
measure, where the data comes from, how each figure will say what it is and how the prototype
was evaluated.

---

## What will the section be?

A quick, explained reading of where the Portuguese economy stands: activity, the labour
market, inflation and recession risk. It will not be a GDP forecast and it does not replace
Banco de Portugal or INE, which remain the official sources.

The principle: **every figure will say what it is and what it is not**. When a result is
modest, we will say so; when it is provisional, it will be marked provisional; when a model
loses to a simple benchmark, the benchmark is what we publish.

---

## Where does the data come from?

The prototype used about **210 public series**, all from official or open sources:

- **BPstat (Banco de Portugal)**: coincident activity, credit, deposits, interest rates,
  financial stress and wages;
- **Eurostat**: national accounts (GDP), industrial production, retail trade, prices, the
  labour market and the European Commission's confidence surveys (consumers, industry,
  services, construction and retail, published at the end of each month);
- **FRED / ALFRED**: including the historical versions of Portuguese GDP, which let an
  estimate be scored against the value as it was first published;
- **Google Trends**: search series;
- **ECB, REN, ENTSO-E, IMF and others**: policy rates, electricity consumption and economic
  policy uncertainty.

The transformations (year-on-year and quarter-on-quarter changes, adjustments) are automatic
and reproducible; no data is edited by hand.

---

## How will each figure say what it is?

Each figure will carry one of four labels, with a fixed meaning:

1. **Official data**: a value published by an official source (INE, Eurostat, Banco de
   Portugal, IEFP), reproduced as is, with the date it refers to. It is not ours.
2. **Reading**: a summary of official data already published (the activity pulse, the labour
   market, current recession risk). It describes the present; it is not a forecast.
3. **Indicative estimate**: a model estimate of a value not yet released (GDP growth in the
   current quarter). It is never authoritative: the official number is INE's.
4. **Forecast**: a simulated distribution of future outcomes (how the year could end). It will
   always be published as a distribution: read the intervals, not just the central point.

## How was the prototype evaluated?

- **As if in real time.** We replayed history from 2005: in each quarter, the model only sees
  what would have been published by then. One caveat: most series have no archive of past
  versions, so we used today's revised values, cut off by their publication date. That tends
  to make the model look better than it would have been in real time. The confidence
  surveys, which carry much of the signal, are hardly ever revised.
- **Against the first release.** Where past versions exist, we scored each estimate against
  GDP as it was first published, not against today's value, which the model could never
  have known.
- **Against a fair benchmark.** The benchmark is an AR(1): a simple model that estimates the
  quarter from the previous one, with exactly the same information.
- **Intervals from our own errors.** The intervals do not come from the model's theory, which
  made them two to three times too narrow, but from the distribution of the errors the
  prototype made in the past at each point of the quarter.

## What did the internal test show?

These results come from an internal test run on the data available up to July 2026. They are
not published forecasts and will be redone before launch. The relative error compares the
model's error with the AR(1)'s: 0.90 means an error about 10% smaller.

- **In the first month of the quarter**, a combination of two models had a relative error of
  about 0.90.
- **In the second month**, the same combination with an economic sentiment indicator had
  about 0.81, a **provisional** result. That indicator was the only survivor of a contest
  with 19 candidates and a selection rule fixed in advance. The raw p-value of the
  Diebold–Mariano test was 0.014, but because it was the best of 19, the selection-adjusted
  p-value is about 0.06 to 0.1: **above 0.05, so it is not statistically significant at the
  conventional level**. It was written down before the results that if the advantage does
  not hold in the following quarters, the indicator goes.
- **At the end of the quarter**, the relative error was 0.775, a modest advantage that is not
  significant at 5% (p-value about 0.23) and rests partly on the 2021 rebound.
- **Part of the advantage is just tracking the level.** Over the last decade, a simple moving
  average of the last 12 quarters captures most of the gain over the AR(1).
- **In the first quarter scored after the selection (2026 Q1), every model lost to the
  AR(1).** The 80% interval covered the observed value at all three points of the quarter;
  in the second month, only just.

## Why will quarterly GDP not be the headline figure?

The first GDP release is itself an estimate. Between INE's flash estimate and the final
values, revisions have a typical error of about 0.3 percentage points. No estimate of final
GDP can be more certain than that. So when the section is published, it will lead with
readings of official data and treat the current-quarter GDP estimate as its least
authoritative figure.

---

## Which limits do we accept?

- There are few quarters to evaluate (about 80, excluding the pandemic), so many honest
  results are "modest, not significant".
- Without past versions of most data, the evaluation only approximates real time.
- Estimates for the current quarter will be indicative, never authoritative.
- What does not survive evaluation goes: models that looked good were withdrawn when their
  numbers did not reproduce.

## Glossary

- **AR(1)**: a simple model that estimates the next quarter from the previous one. It is the
  benchmark any model has to beat.
- **Relative error**: the model's root mean squared error (RMSE) divided by the AR(1)'s.
  Below 1, the model errs less; 0.90 means an error about 10% smaller.
- **Diebold–Mariano test and p-value**: a test of whether the difference in errors between
  two models could be chance. A p-value below 0.05 is the conventional threshold for saying
  it is not.
- **First, second and third month**: the point in the quarter at which the estimate is made.
  The later it is, the more official data is already out.
- **Economic sentiment indicator**: the European Commission's index that summarises the
  confidence surveys.
- **80% interval**: the range in which, in the normal quarters of the past, the official
  value fell eight times in ten.
