# How the synthetic population was made

This page explains, for a general reader, why a synthetic population is needed, how the Synthetic Population of Portugal (release <Release />) was generated, what it can and cannot be used for, and where its limits are. The full technical account is the <ModelCardLink>model card</ModelCardLink>.

## Generated people, not real ones

A synthetic population is a set of computer-generated people and households, one record per person, whose totals follow the tables INE published from the 2021 Census for each parish. No record is a real person. It can serve as a foundation layer for demographic analysis, but it does not replace official statistics.

## Why a synthetic population is needed

Picture a small parish with 10 homes and 23 people. In the 2021 Census, each person answered questions about their age, their education, their work and who they live with. INE keeps those answers, one by one.

</Beat>

<Beat at="count">

But it does not publish them that way: it protects the people who answered (statistical confidentiality). What leaves INE is counts, table by table: how many people there are in each age group, how many households of each size. In this parish, 3 people live alone and 8 are aged 65 or over. Who lives in which home, and with whom, is left out.

</Beat>

<Beat at="question">

Many questions cross two tables. How many of the 8 people aged 65 or over live alone? One table says 3 people live alone, the other that 8 are aged 65 or over; neither says how many are both. INE publishes some cross-tabulations by parish, but not all: there would be too many tables, with many cells of one or two people.

</Beat>

<Beat at="ambiguity">

And arithmetic on the tables is not enough. The same 23 people fit into the same 10 homes in many ways, and every one of them gives exactly the same tables. In one, nobody aged 65 or over lives alone; in another, 3 of them do. The tables on their own cannot choose between them.

</Beat>

<Beat at="sample">

INE also publishes a sample of answers, the 2021 Census Public Use File: about 1 in 20 households in the country, with each person's answers kept together, but without the parish (only the region, NUTS II). The sample shows how people live together; the tables, how many there are in each parish. Neither, on its own, gives the people of a parish.

A synthetic population joins the two: households that look like the sample's, chosen so that their counts give each parish's tables. With it, any cross-tabulation is a matter of counting people.

</Beat>

<Beat at="learn">

## Five steps to generate it

### 1. Learn how attributes go together

The computer learns, from that sample, which combinations of age, education, work and family are common in a person and their household. That model (an autoregressive generator) is trained once and then frozen. Single-year ages and the type of workplace are generated by it, not derived afterwards.

The generator writes each household like a sentence, one attribute at a time, and every choice reads everything already written: the second person's age tends to stay close to the first's, and a four-year-old cannot have a degree or a job.

</Beat>

<Beat at="candidates">

### 2. Fit each parish to its tables

The result does not come straight out of the generator: it invents possible people and households, and then the ones whose totals come as close as possible to INE's tables are chosen (a constrained generative process). For the invented parish it invents 24 candidate households, all with the same weight. The bars show what they add up to; the black squares, what the parish's tables ask for: 10 households and 23 people.

</Beat>

<Beat at="mask">

The model avoids the combinations INE publishes as zero in that parish (a mask). This one has no households of 5 or more people, so 3 candidates are left out. The mask restricts the candidates, but the result can still leave some people in those combinations: in the fitted person tables, about 7,900 entries (see "Where the data are weakest").

</Beat>

<Beat at="tilt">

It then gives more weight to the candidates that bring the totals closer to INE's tables for the parish, changing each one's weight as little as possible (a maximum-entropy tilt). Before that, a check would refuse any parish whose tables imply totals that are inconsistent with one another; none of the 3,092 was refused.

Here the candidates came out too young for the parish: as you scroll, the households of people aged 65 or over grow, the young couples shrink and the bars reach the squares.

</Beat>

<Beat at="choose">

### 3. Draw whole households

A person cannot be split: whole households are drawn from the candidates, as close as possible to the counts the parish's tables ask for (an integer allocation). In parishes of under 500 people that draw is solved to the optimum (an exact selection, with no approximation), which still does not guarantee INE's total. <AllocationGap />

Here 10 of the 21 candidates stay, the household written in step 1 among them, and the sums match both tables exactly.

</Beat>

<Beat at="answer">

Now the opening question has an answer, and it only takes counting: in this population, 3 of the 8 people aged 65 or over live alone. The tables only guaranteed that the answer lay between 0 and 3; the number comes from what the generator learned from the sample about who lives with whom.

In the parish we began with, it was 2. The synthetic population gives a possible answer, consistent with the tables, not the true one, and for a real parish we do not have the true one to compare. That is why the site says, for every answer, whether it comes from a field fitted to the tables or from a cross-tabulation no table in the fit checks (see "Fitted fields and derived fields"), and why every parish has a quality tier. This is how [each parish's page](/en/populacao/) answers this very question.

</Beat>

<Beat at="collective">

### 4. Add the residents of collective quarters

People living in care homes and other collective quarters are appended after the fit, from INE's published counts, as partial records: sex and age come from those counts, education is imputed from similar records, and the family nucleus and the work fields are left empty (the [column dictionary](/en/populacao/dados/#dicionario) says which). Fine occupation codes are derived at this stage.

In the invented parish, besides the 10 homes, INE counts 5 people in a care home.

</Beat>

<Beat at="publish">

### 5. Publish with a declared schema and a quality tier

Every published column has a declared meaning, and the [column dictionary](/en/populacao/dados/#dicionario) explains each one; everything else is dropped, with the reason recorded. The release carries no link between partners. Each person takes one row, and every column says where it comes from.

</Beat>

<Beat at="tier">

Each parish comes out with a quality tier, A, B or C (see "Every parish answers, with a quality tier"). With 28 residents, the invented parish is tier C, like every parish under 500.

</Beat>

## Fitted fields and derived fields

The figures on this site use nine fields. Six are groupings of a generated field whose INE table is in the fit (five-year age bands group the generated single-year age, and the age bands join those groups); two are derived by a fixed rule and no table in the fit checks them (for example, "lives alone" means the household has one person); and one is a flag. The table gives, for each, the microdata column or the rule that computes it from the columns, with the same letters as the column dictionary.

<ProvenanceFields />

The household questions (people per household, family nuclei, several generations), "who lives alone" and "people aged 65 or over who live alone" count private households only, INE's household universe: people living in a care home or other collective living quarters are not part of them.

In the downloadable microdata, each column also carries its provenance: generated by the model (G), derived from another column (D), made consistent after generation (C), or assigned from the geographic code (X). The [column dictionary](/en/populacao/dados/#dicionario) explains each one, and the [quality page](/en/populacao/qualidade/#tabelas) says which tables were fitted and which were only scored.

To see the idea with invented people, in an imagined neighbourhood, there is an [illustrated example](/en/populacao/miniatura/). It uses no data from this population.

## Every parish answers, with a quality tier

Always. All 3,092 parishes are published, and every one answers every question with its own figures. Each has a quality tier, which combines how close the generated population comes to INE's tables and the number of residents. The tier is a reading guide, not a filter: it hides no figure, it says how carefully to read it. A small parish has few people in each table, and each person weighs more.

Parishes with fewer than 500 residents always sit in tier C. Among the tier C parishes of 500 or more, the worst table is almost always single-year age, which is scored but is not one of the fitted person tables, and which the site's answers do not use: in those parishes the tier C caution applies mostly to anyone using single-year age from the microdata. Each parish page says what put it in its tier. The terms used to measure the fit (typical error, worst table) are explained, with an example, in the [quality page's glossary](/en/populacao/qualidade/#glossario).

## A single run: no intervals, no rankings

<SingleRun />

That is why the site's figures carry no intervals, no rankings of parishes and no "more than" comparisons between results. A second run will follow publication, as a measurement; from two runs onward, the variation between them can be shown again. Even then, we will only call it a confidence interval if its coverage is demonstrated.

## The privacy of the people who answered the Census

The records are generated and are not intended to represent identifiable people: they carry no names or addresses, and the internal pointers to sample records have been dropped.

## Corrections and new releases

- The releases published on GitHub (1.0.0, 1.0.1 and 1.0.3) stay available and citable; 1.0.2 was never published. Each answer's permanent link names the release it refers to; a parish page always shows the current release, so cite it with the date you read it.
- Corrections are recorded in a public <ErrataLink>errata log</ErrataLink>.
- A revision of INE's sources triggers a documented patch or a new evaluation.
- The next release (1.1) will be a corrected synthetic population for 2021. A later release, with 2025 data, will say which fields were updated to 2025, which were carried forward from 2021, and which are newly modelled.
- The model weights are not published: that would require its own memorisation, privacy and licence review.
